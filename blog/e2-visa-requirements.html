E-2 Visa Requirements: The Five Things You Need to Qualify

By Emmanuel Okharedia • April 2026 • 13 min read

The E-2 Treaty Investor Visa has five core requirements. Miss any one of them and the application fails, regardless of how strong the other elements are. Most denials come not from an investor doing something wrong, but from misunderstanding what each requirement actually demands.

This article walks through all five requirements in detail. It covers what the law says, what consular officers are actually looking for, and where applicants most often stumble.

The requirements come from the statute at INA 101(a)(15)(E), the regulations at 8 CFR 214.2(e), and the State Department's instructions to consular officers at 9 FAM 402.9. Here is the whole framework at a glance:

Requirement One: Treaty Country Nationality

You must be a citizen of a country that has a qualifying trade and commerce treaty with the United States. The U.S. maintains these treaties with more than 80 countries. Your nationality at the time of application controls, not your country of residence.

This requirement is binary. Either your country has a treaty or it does not. If it does not, no amount of money or business experience changes your eligibility. Citizens of China, India, Brazil, Russia, Vietnam, and Nigeria are among the most common nationalities that cannot use the E-2 program.

The 50 percent rule for company nationality

The nationality requirement applies to the enterprise, not just to you. A company takes the nationality of its owners, and to qualify, at least 50 percent of the business must be owned by nationals of the treaty country. If you own the whole company, this is automatic. If you have partners, the math becomes a threshold issue: a British citizen who owns half of a U.S. LLC alongside an American partner has a qualifying British enterprise, but if that share drops to 40 percent, the company loses its treaty nationality and the E-2 fails. Any buy-in or restructuring that dilutes treaty ownership below half puts the visa itself at stake.

One technical point: a treaty-country citizen who holds a U.S. green card does not count toward the 50 percent. For this calculation, that owner's stake is treated as domestic.

Dual citizens and permanent residents

If you hold dual citizenship, things get more interesting. If one of your nationalities is a treaty country, you may be able to apply through that nationality, even if your other passport is from a non-treaty country. You choose which passport to apply under, and the enterprise's nationality follows the citizenship you use: a person holding both Indian and Canadian citizenship simply applies as a Canadian. See the full breakdown in E-2 Treaty Countries: The Complete List and What It Means for You.

Permanent residency, by contrast, is never enough. Someone who has lived in the United Kingdom for twenty years on permanent resident status but still carries a Nigerian passport is a Nigerian national for E-2 purposes. The treaty benefit attaches to citizenship only, and until the citizenship certificate is in hand, the E-2 door stays closed.

One important nuance: the nationality that controls is the one you use to apply. You need to demonstrate ties to the treaty country and typically apply at that country's consulate.

Requirement Two: A Substantial Investment

The word "substantial" is doing a lot of work here, and the law deliberately avoids setting a specific dollar figure. The State Department evaluates substantiality in two ways: proportionality and sufficiency.

Proportionality means your investment is a significant percentage of the total cost to acquire or establish the business. If the total cost is $100,000, putting in $80,000 is proportional. Putting in $20,000 is not, even if $20,000 is a lot of money in absolute terms.

Sufficiency means the amount is enough to ensure the business's successful operation. A business plan that requires $150,000 to launch properly, supported by an investment of $60,000, raises immediate questions.

The sliding scale

Proportionality operates as a sliding scale, and 9 FAM 402.9 describes it in exactly those terms: the lower the total cost of the business, the higher the percentage you are expected to fund. For a business that costs $100,000 to establish or acquire, an officer will generally want to see 80 to 100 percent of that amount invested. A $500,000 business might qualify with a somewhat lower proportion, and a multi-million dollar enterprise with a lower percentage still, because at that scale the absolute amount is unmistakably substantial on its own. Partial funding with a promise to invest more later reads as hedging.

In practice, successful E-2 investments typically fall between $80,000 and $300,000. Investments below $50,000 are very rarely approved. Higher-cost businesses, such as manufacturing operations or full-service restaurants, often require significantly more. Read the deeper analysis in How Much Do You Need to Invest for an E-2 Visa.

Requirement Three: The Investment Must Be at Risk

This is the requirement that most surprises people. Having the money is not enough. The money must be irrevocably committed to the business enterprise.

What does "irrevocably committed" actually mean? It means the funds have already been deployed in a way you cannot simply reverse. Examples include:

  • Purchase price paid for an existing business or franchise
  • Funds placed in an escrow account tied to a purchase agreement with specific release conditions
  • Money spent on equipment, leasehold improvements, inventory, or startup costs
  • Loans you have personally guaranteed, secured by personal assets

What does not count: money sitting in a business bank account that you could withdraw tomorrow, personal savings earmarked but not yet spent, or a letter of intent to invest that has no binding obligation attached.

The "at risk" requirement also means the investment must be subject to partial or total loss if the business fails. You need to be exposed to real commercial risk. A fully collateralized loan where you face no downside does not satisfy this element.

Here is how the line gets drawn in practice:

Counts toward the investmentDoes not count
Purchase price actually paid for an existing business or franchiseCash sitting in a personal or business account that you could withdraw tomorrow
Equipment, furniture, vehicles, and technology bought for the businessA letter of intent or unsigned agreement with no binding obligation
Leasehold improvements and build-out costsFunds earmarked for the business but never actually spent
Inventory and initial suppliesValue you assign to your own ideas, time, or goodwill
Escrowed funds under a binding agreement with release conditioned only on visa issuanceEscrowed funds you can recover at will, with no binding release conditions
Loans secured by your personal assets, where you carry the repayment riskLoans secured only by the assets of the E-2 business itself
Reasonable startup expenses: licenses, insurance, deposits, initial marketingFully collateralized borrowing where the lender's only recourse is business assets and you face no personal downside

The escrow entry matters because it is the standard tool for buying an existing business without gambling the purchase price on visa approval. A binding escrow agreement, with release conditioned only on visa issuance, satisfies the at-risk test while protecting you if the application is denied.

Source of funds: proving where the money came from

Committing the money is half the battle; you also have to trace it. Officers want a documented path from the origin of the funds to the business bank account, and gaps in that chain generate refusals.

Legitimate sources include earnings and savings (shown through employment records, tax returns, and bank statements over time), sale of property (shown through the sale contract and closing statement), sale of another business, inheritance, and gifts. A gifted investment is acceptable if documented: a signed gift letter stating the funds carry no repayment obligation, plus evidence of the donor's own lawful source of funds.

Borrowed funds follow the at-risk logic above. A loan secured by your personal assets, such as a mortgage on your home, counts because you carry the repayment risk personally. A loan secured by the assets of the E-2 business itself does not. How you assemble and paper these funds is a planning exercise in its own right; see How to Structure Your E-2 Investment.

Requirement Four: A Real and Non-Marginal Enterprise

This is where many applications run into trouble, and it is worth spending time on.

A marginal enterprise is defined by regulations as one that does not have the present or future capacity to generate more than enough income to provide a minimal living for the investor and their family. The E-2 is designed for businesses that contribute to the U.S. economy, not just support the investor's lifestyle.

What makes a business non-marginal?

The clearest indicator is employment. A business that creates jobs for U.S. workers is almost by definition non-marginal. The regulations specify that "U.S. workers" means people who are not the investor or the investor's immediate family.

If a business does not currently employ U.S. workers, it can still qualify by demonstrating the capacity to do so in the future. A credible five-year business plan showing projected hiring, based on realistic revenue projections, can satisfy this requirement for a new enterprise.

A solo consulting firm with no employees and no plans to hire is the classic problem case. Even with a significant investment, it is very difficult to argue that such a business is non-marginal.

A worked example: solo consultant vs. cafe

Suppose two applicants each invest $120,000. The first is a management consultant who forms a U.S. LLC, works alone from a home office, and projects $95,000 in annual net income. The money is real, but the enterprise's only economic function is to support the consultant. No hiring, no payroll, no footprint beyond one person's living. Expect the officer to conclude the business is marginal.

The second applicant opens a cafe with the same $120,000. Year one projections show the owner plus two part-time staff; by the end of year two, the plan calls for four employees on payroll, backed by realistic revenue figures, a signed lease, and comparable financials from similar cafes. This business creates jobs, pays payroll taxes, and buys from local suppliers. That is what non-marginal looks like, and the difference has nothing to do with the size of the check.

A concrete, credible plan to hire or subcontract meaningful work to U.S. workers can still move the analysis for the consultant, but a hiring projection bolted on just to make the numbers work is easy to spot.

The five-year window for new businesses

For a new enterprise, the regulations give you time. A business that cannot yet generate non-marginal income qualifies if it has the capacity to do so within five years of the visa being issued. That capacity is proven through the business plan: revenue projections built from defensible assumptions and a hiring timeline tied to those revenues. The test is only as strong as the projections behind it.

Learn more about which types of businesses satisfy this standard in What Businesses Qualify for an E-2 Visa.

Requirement Five: You Are Coming to Develop and Direct the Business

The E-2 is not a passive investor visa. You must be coming to the United States to actively manage the enterprise. This typically means one of two things.

First, you own at least 50 percent of the business. At that ownership level, you have inherent control over business decisions, and the presumption is that you are the one directing operations.

Second, if you own less than 50 percent, you must hold a position within the company that gives you operational control. This is harder to demonstrate and requires careful documentation of your role relative to other owners.

Minority owners are not shut out, but they have to build control through corporate devices. An operating agreement naming you as sole managing member, a management agreement vesting day-to-day authority in your position, or voting arrangements giving you decision-making power over operations can each establish the required control. The documents have to show that you, not your co-owners, actually run the enterprise. A 30 percent owner with no special governance rights does not develop and direct anything.

Employees of the E-2 investor can also obtain E-2 status if they hold the same treaty nationality as the enterprise and serve in executive, supervisory, or essential skills positions. An essential skills employee brings specialized knowledge the business cannot readily source in the U.S. labor market. This is separate from the investor's own application and has its own requirements.

How the Requirements Work Together

Here is the thing about E-2 requirements: they are evaluated holistically, not as a checklist. A consular officer is making a judgment about whether your investment, your business, and your intentions add up to a coherent picture.

A very large investment in an otherwise weak business plan does not guarantee approval. A well-documented, modest investment in a carefully structured business can succeed. The story has to be complete and consistent across every document in the application package.

Common patterns that lead to problems include:

  • Investment funds that cannot be traced clearly to a legitimate source
  • Business plans that lack specificity about operations, hiring, and revenue projections
  • Investments that appear staged or conditional rather than genuinely committed
  • Businesses that are essentially freelance or consulting arrangements dressed up as companies

Understanding why applications fail is just as important as understanding what makes them succeed. See Common Reasons E-2 Visa Applications Are Denied for a frank look at the most frequent problems.

Documentation: Turning Requirements into Evidence

Each requirement must be proven with documentation, not just stated. The application package for an E-2 petition typically runs 200 to 400 pages. That is not padding. Every page is serving a purpose.

Treaty nationality is established through your passport. The investment amount and at-risk status are demonstrated through bank records, wire transfers, purchase agreements, escrow documents, expense receipts, and lease agreements. The non-marginal enterprise is shown through a business plan, financial projections, and, for existing businesses, tax returns and payroll records. Your role is documented through the organizational structure, operating agreement, and your biography.

The source of your investment funds also requires documentation. You need to show not just that you have the money, but where it came from. Unexplained large deposits, funds from unrelated third parties, or capital without a clear origin can all raise red flags at the interview stage.

Where You Prove It: Consulate or USCIS

The five requirements never change, but there are two procedural routes for demonstrating them.

Consular processing is the standard route. You complete the DS-160 online application along with the DS-156E treaty investor supplement, submit your evidence package to the U.S. embassy or consulate, and attend an in-person interview. The consular officer applies the five requirements directly at that interview, so preparation for that conversation matters as much as the paperwork; see How to Prepare for Your E-2 Visa Interview. Approval results in a visa in your passport that lets you travel in and out of the United States.

Change of status is available if you are already in the United States in another lawful status. You file Form I-129 with USCIS, and the adjudication happens on paper, with no interview. If approved, you hold E-2 status and can run the business, but you do not receive a visa. The moment you leave the country, you must apply at a consulate anyway to return, and the consular officer is not bound by the USCIS approval. Change of status suits people who need to start operating quickly and can defer travel.

Key takeaway: All five requirements must be satisfied simultaneously. Strong performance on four out of five is not enough. The application needs to tell a complete, documented story on every element, which is why thorough preparation matters so much.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I qualify for an E-2 visa with less than $50,000?

Rarely. There is no statutory minimum, but the proportionality and sufficiency tests are very hard to satisfy at that level. An investment under $50,000 implies either a business too small to be credible or one that is significantly underfunded. Most successful E-2 investments fall between $80,000 and $300,000.

Does buying a franchise satisfy the requirements?

A franchise can be an excellent E-2 vehicle, but it does not automatically satisfy anything. The franchise fee, build-out, and equipment purchases are clearly documented at-risk expenditures, and the franchisor's proven model makes projections more credible. You still face the full substantiality, marginality, and develop-and-direct analysis, and a franchise handed to a manager while you stay abroad fails like any other passive investment.

Can my spouse work on an E-2 visa?

Yes. Your spouse receives work authorization incident to status and can work for any employer in the United States, in any field. Children under 21 can live in the U.S. and attend school, but they cannot work. The full picture for dependents is covered in The E-2 Visa for Your Family: Spouses and Children.

How long is the E-2 visa valid?

It depends on the reciprocity schedule between the United States and your treaty country. Many countries receive visas valid for up to five years with multiple entries; others receive far shorter periods. Each entry grants a two-year period of status, and there is no cap on renewals: as long as the business operates and the requirements remain satisfied, the E-2 can be extended indefinitely.

Do I need to hire employees immediately?

No. A new enterprise qualifies by showing the capacity to become non-marginal within five years, normally through a business plan with realistic hiring projections. What you cannot do is present a plan that never involves hiring anyone, and at renewal the officer will compare projections against reality.

Can I apply if my country is not a treaty country?

Not directly. Treaty nationality is a strict citizenship requirement with no waiver. Some investors acquire citizenship in a treaty country first and apply as nationals of that country. Others use the L-1 intracompany transferee visa, which has no nationality restriction but requires an existing foreign company transferring you to a U.S. affiliate.

What happens to my E-2 if I sell the business?

Your status is tied to the specific qualifying enterprise, so selling it ends the basis for your E-2. The practical options are to depart, change to another nonimmigrant status, or invest in a new qualifying enterprise and file a fresh application. If a sale is on the horizon, plan the immigration sequence before you sign.
