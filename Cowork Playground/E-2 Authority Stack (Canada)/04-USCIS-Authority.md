## 1. USCIS Policy Manual, Volume 2 (Nonimmigrants), Part G - Treaty Traders and Treaty Investors (E-1, E-2)

Source URL: https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-2-part-g
Parent URL (Volume 2): https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-2
Table of contents: https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/table-of-contents

Status as of pull date: This is the live, controlling USCIS guidance on E-1 and E-2 adjudications by USCIS officers (i.e., petitions and change-of-status / extension-of-stay requests filed on Form I-129 inside the United States). It does not bind consular officers, who follow 9 FAM 402.9, but USCIS Policy Manual Part G and 9 FAM 402.9 are substantively aligned on the core eligibility standards.

Coverage of E classification in the Policy Manual as of 2026: USCIS has fully migrated E-1/E-2 coverage from the retired Adjudicator's Field Manual into Volume 2, Part G of the Policy Manual. Part G is organized into chapters that cover purpose and background, treaty country and nationality, the substantive eligibility standards for E-1 (trade) and E-2 (investment), E-employee qualifications (executive/supervisory or essential skills), and procedural matters (filing, period of stay, extension, change of status, employment authorization for derivatives). The Policy Manual now serves as the authoritative single source on the USCIS side; legacy AFM Chapter 32 (E classification material) has been crosswalked into this Part G.

Substantive content captured from USCIS Policy Manual, Volume 2, Part G (compiled summary; verify against the live URL above before filing):

### A. Treaty country and nationality

In all treaty investor cases, it must be shown that nationals of a treaty country own at least 50 percent of an enterprise. It must also be shown, in accordance with INA 101(a)(15)(E)(ii), that a national or nationals of the treaty country, through ownership or by other means, develops and directs the activities of the enterprise.

If the principal employer is not an individual, it must be an enterprise or organization at least 50 percent owned by persons in the United States who have the nationality of the treaty country. This is established by showing at least 50 percent ownership of the enterprise or possession of operational control through a managerial position or other corporate device. Except where a business is controlled equally by nationals of two treaty countries, a business may only have one nationality for E-2 purposes.

The nationality of a business for E purposes is determined by the nationality of its owners, not by where the business is incorporated.

### B. Investment standards

Investment is the treaty investor's placing of capital, including funds and other assets, at risk in the commercial sense with the objective of generating a profit. The capital must be subject to partial or total loss if the investment fails.

To be "in the process of investing," the funds or assets to be invested must be committed to the investment, and the commitment must be real and irrevocable. Funds are "irrevocably committed" when the investor has taken steps that are legally binding or demonstrate a clear intent to complete the investment, such that the investor would be unable to withdraw the funds at will. Escrow arrangements conditioned on visa issuance can satisfy this if the assets are held in escrow for release or transfer upon condition.

Funds sitting in a business checking account that can be withdrawn at will are not "at risk."

### C. Substantiality

The investment must be substantial in relationship to the total cost of either purchasing an established enterprise or establishing a new one, and of a magnitude to support the likelihood that the treaty investor will successfully develop and direct the enterprise. The lower the cost of the enterprise, the higher, proportionately, the investment must be to be considered substantial. (This embeds the inverted "proportionality test" reflected in 9 FAM 402.9-6(D) and the regulations.)

### D. Bona fide enterprise and the no-marginality rule

A bona fide enterprise refers to a real, active, and operating commercial or entrepreneurial undertaking which produces services or goods for profit. It must meet applicable legal requirements for doing business within its jurisdiction. It cannot be a paper organization or an idle speculative investment held for potential appreciation in value, such as undeveloped land or stocks held by an investor without the intent to direct the enterprise.

The investment enterprise may not be marginal. A marginal enterprise is one that does not have the present or future capacity to generate more than enough income to provide a minimal living for the treaty investor and his or her family. Marginality is typically rebutted by demonstrating either present non-marginal income or a five-year projection showing capacity to generate more than minimal income (or, alternatively, a significant economic contribution).

### E. Develop and direct

The E-2 investor must be coming to the United States solely to develop and direct the operations of the enterprise. This is established by showing at least 50 percent ownership of the enterprise or possession of operational control through a managerial position or other corporate device.

### F. Treaty employee qualifications (E-1 and E-2 employees)

To qualify for E-2 classification, the employee of a treaty investor must:

- Be the same nationality as the principal alien employer (i.e., the nationality of the qualifying business);
- Meet the definition of "employee" under relevant law (control test);
- Either be engaging in duties of an executive or supervisory character, or, if employed in a lesser capacity, have special qualifications that make the employee's services essential to the efficient operation of the enterprise.

Duties of an executive or supervisory character are those which primarily provide the employee ultimate control and responsibility for the enterprise's overall operation or a major component of it.

Special (essential) qualifications are skills and aptitudes which make the employee's services essential to the efficient operation of the enterprise. Factors include: degree of proven expertise; whether others possess the employee's specific skills; the salary the special qualifications can command; and whether the skills and qualifications are readily available in the United States. Knowledge of a foreign language and culture does not, by itself, meet this requirement. A skill that is essential at one point in time may become commonplace, and therefore no longer qualifying, at a later date.

An E-2 employee may also work for the treaty organization's parent company or one of its subsidiaries, provided the relationship is established, the subsidiary employment requires executive, supervisory, or essential skills, and terms and conditions of employment have not otherwise changed.

### G. Period of stay; extension of stay; change of status

Qualified treaty investors and employees are admitted for a maximum initial period of two years. Extensions of stay and changes of status to E-2 may be granted in increments of up to two years each. There is no limit to the number of extensions.

E nonimmigrants who travel abroad may generally be granted, if determined admissible by CBP, an automatic two-year period of readmission when returning to the United States, even without a separately approved Form I-129.

All E nonimmigrants must maintain an intention to depart the United States when their status expires or is terminated (no presumption against intent rule; dual intent is not formally recognized for E but the intent to depart is satisfied by a credible declaration of intent rather than evidence of nonabandonment of foreign domicile).

### H. Adjudication record for USCIS purposes

USCIS adjudicates Form I-129 with the E-1/E-2 Classification Supplement when the applicant is in the United States and seeks change or extension of status. Initial entry visa applications by Canadians abroad would normally be processed at a U.S. consular post under 9 FAM 402.9; however, Canadians are visa-exempt at ports of entry and may also seek E-2 status by direct filing of Form I-129 with USCIS while present in the U.S., or, for a fresh issuance, may proceed through the consular post (e.g., Toronto, Montreal, Calgary, Vancouver, Ottawa, Quebec City; see file 07-Post-Specific-Canada.md).

### I. Chapter outline (as published by USCIS in Part G)

Per the live Part G table of contents (https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-2-part-g), the part is organized by chapter into purpose/background, eligibility (treaty country, nationality, ownership), the trade requirement (E-1) and investment requirement (E-2) with substantiality and marginality, treaty employees, documentation/evidence, and period of stay/extension/change of status. Practitioners should pull the live chapter text for any filing because USCIS continues to update Part G as additional AFM-era material is incorporated and as new policy guidance issues.

## 2. USCIS Policy Manual, Volume 2, Part A - Nonimmigrant Policies and Procedures (cross-cutting application to E)

Source URL: https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-2-part-a
Specific chapter, extension of stay and change of status (cross-cutting): https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-2-part-a-chapter-4

Volume 2, Part A sets out the cross-cutting USCIS rules for all nonimmigrant categories on filing, evidence, status maintenance, extension of stay, change of status, and extension of petition validity. For E-2 work, the principal cross-cutting chapter is Chapter 4 (Extension of Stay, Change of Status, and Extension of Petition Validity), which governs:

- Eligibility and procedural standards for extension of stay (EOS) filings;
- Eligibility for change of status (COS), including the requirement that the applicant be in valid status at the time of filing and not have engaged in unauthorized employment;
- Bars to EOS/COS (e.g., for visitors in transit, certain S, K, M categories, vessel crew);
- Effects of departure on a pending EOS/COS;
- Adjudication of "split" extensions where part of the requested period has already passed.

For E-2 specifically, Part A Chapter 4 controls the procedural mechanics of any Form I-129 filed inside the United States to extend E-2 or to change to E-2 from another nonimmigrant category.

## 3. USCIS Policy Manual, Volume 10, Part B, Chapter 2 - Employment Authorization for Certain H-4, E, and L Nonimmigrant Dependent Spouses

Source URL: https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-10-part-b-chapter-2

Status: Live, controlling USCIS guidance on the work authorization rules for E dependent spouses.

Key points relevant to E-2 Canada practice:

- Spouses of E-2 workers in valid E-2 or E-2S status are employment authorized incident to status, except for spouses of long-term investors in the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands (E-2 CNMI), who must apply for an EAD under 8 CFR 274a.12(c)(12).
- As of January 30, 2022, USCIS and CBP began issuing Forms I-94 with new Class of Admission (COA) codes for certain E and L spouses: E-1S, E-2S, E-3S, and L-2S. These codes distinguish dependent spouses (who are employment authorized incident to status) from dependent children (who are not).
- An unexpired Form I-94 notated with E-1S, E-2S, E-3S, or L-2S nonimmigrant status is acceptable as List C evidence of employment authorization on Form I-9.
- For spouses with a pre-January 30, 2022 Form I-94 still notated E-1, E-2, E-3, E-3D, E-3R, or L-2, USCIS has been issuing supplementary notices that the holder is identified as an employment-authorized spouse; the notice paired with the Form I-94 functions as evidence of employment authorization.
- The classification was confirmed by USCIS in its November 2021 Policy Alert and codified in this Volume 10, Part B, Chapter 2 guidance.

## 4. Form I-129, Petition for a Nonimmigrant Worker, and Instructions

Form URL: https://www.uscis.gov/i-129
Form PDF (current edition 02/27/26): https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/forms/i-129.pdf
Instructions PDF: https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/forms/i-129instr.pdf
Direct filing addresses: https://www.uscis.gov/i-129-addresses
Required initial evidence checklist: https://www.uscis.gov/i-129Checklist
Fee schedule (G-1055): https://www.uscis.gov/g-1055

### Form description

Form I-129 is used by petitioners to file on behalf of a nonimmigrant worker to come to the United States temporarily to perform services or labor, or to receive training, as an H-1B, H-2A, H-2B, H-3, L-1, O-1, O-2, P-1, P-1S, P-2, P-2S, P-3, P-3S, Q-1, or R-1 nonimmigrant worker. Petitioners may also use this form to request an extension of stay in or change of status to E-1, E-2, E-3, H-1B1, or TN classification, or one of the above classifications for an alien.

### Current edition and effective date (2026)

USCIS published a new edition of Form I-129 on February 27, 2026 (edition date 02/27/26). Starting April 1, 2026, USCIS will accept only the 02/27/26 edition. Confirm the accepted edition at the I-129 form page before filing.

### Fees

The 2026 Fee Schedule (Form G-1055), published May 6, 2026, includes the online filing fee for Form I-129 across E-1, E-2, E-2C, R, and TN classifications. For exact dollar amounts by classification and employer size (small employer / nonprofit / standard), use the USCIS Fee Calculator at https://www.uscis.gov/feecalculator or G-1055 directly. Asylum Program Fee and the small-employer / nonprofit tiering apply to E filings, and the per-form fee differs between paper and online filing.

### Key filing requirements (E-2 inside the U.S.)

- File Form I-129 with the E-1/E-2 Classification Supplement (Supplement E) and required initial evidence;
- Demonstrate that the applicant (and any qualifying employee) meets the E-2 nationality, ownership/control, investment, and substantiality/marginality requirements;
- For extension of stay or change of status filings, applicant must be in lawful status at the time of filing and must not have engaged in unauthorized employment;
- File at the direct filing address designated for E classifications (see https://www.uscis.gov/i-129-addresses);
- Premium processing is available for E-1, E-2, and E-3 petitions under the premium processing program. See https://www.uscis.gov/forms/all-forms/h-and-l-filing-fees-for-form-i-129-petition-for-a-nonimmigrant-worker and the I-907 page for current premium processing fees and timeframes.

### Form I-129 instructions text (substantive excerpts as applicable to E)

The Form I-129 instructions include category-specific instructions for E-1 (treaty trader) and E-2 (treaty investor). The E instructions direct petitioners to:

- Complete the basic petition (Parts 1 through 9) and the E-1/E-2 Classification Supplement;
- Submit evidence that the trade or investment qualifies under INA 101(a)(15)(E) and 8 CFR 214.2(e), including evidence of treaty country nationality, ownership/control, substantial investment (E-2) or substantial trade principally between the U.S. and the treaty country (E-1), bona fide enterprise, and non-marginality (E-2);
- For an E employee, submit evidence that the employee is the same nationality as the qualifying enterprise and either has executive/supervisory duties or special qualifications essential to the efficient operation of the enterprise;
- Submit Form G-28 if represented by counsel;
- Pay the applicable fees, including the Asylum Program Fee where required.

Practitioners pulling the live instructions should also review the "What Evidence Must You Submit" and "Specific Requirements" sections for E classifications, which mirror the substantive standards in Policy Manual Part G.

## 5. Form I-129 E-1/E-2 Classification Supplement (Supplement E)

The E-1/E-2 Classification Supplement is the dedicated supplement page within Form I-129 used for all E-1, E-2, and E-3 filings. It captures classification-specific information beyond what the base I-129 collects.

### What Supplement E asks for

- Identification of the employer and the beneficiary.
- Whether the employer is a U.S. or foreign employer (E classifications allow foreign employers, unlike most other I-129 categories).
- The principal trade or investment country (treaty country).
- Whether the petition is a new office, treaty country trade percentage (for E-1), or an investment described in dollar terms (for E-2).
- The number of executive and supervisory employees the U.S. enterprise has who are nationals of the treaty country in E nonimmigrant status.
- The number of persons with special qualifications essential to the successful or efficient operation of the U.S. enterprise who are employed in E nonimmigrant status.
- If qualifying the applicant as an executive or supervisor, the total number of employees he or she will supervise.

### Initial evidence to file with Supplement E

For E-1 (trade): evidence that the trader will carry on substantial trade principally between the United States and the alien's country of nationality, or, in the case of an E-1 employee, evidence that the employee will principally and primarily perform executive or supervisory duties or possesses special qualifications essential to the enterprise.

For E-2 (investment): evidence that the E-2 investor will develop and direct the operations of a U.S. enterprise. This typically includes proof of nationality, ownership and source/path of funds (including legal source documentation), evidence that the funds are committed and at risk, business plan (often a five-year plan addressing marginality), proof of business operations (lease, EIN, payroll, contracts), and corporate documents establishing 50 percent ownership or operational control by the treaty country national.

### When Supplement E is required

The Supplement is required for any I-129 seeking E-1, E-2, or E-3 classification (initial, extension, change of status, or amended petition) and must be completed even where the foreign national is outside the United States, if the petitioner has elected to file with USCIS rather than apply directly to a consular post.

## 6. USCIS Web Page: E-2 Treaty Investors

Source URL: https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/temporary-workers/e-2-treaty-investors

Status: USCIS's public-facing overview page on the E-2 classification. Authoritative summary that mirrors the substantive standards in Policy Manual Volume 2, Part G.

### Overview (full pulled summary)

The E-2 nonimmigrant classification allows a national of a treaty country (a country with which the United States maintains a treaty of commerce and navigation, or with which the United States maintains a qualifying international agreement, or which has been deemed a qualifying country by legislation) to be admitted to the United States when investing a substantial amount of capital in a U.S. business. Certain employees of such a person or of a qualifying organization may also be eligible for this classification.

For a list of participating countries see U.S. Department of State's Treaty Countries (see file 06-Treaty-Canada.md). Canada is a treaty country (Treaty of Friendship, Commerce, and Navigation legacy / USMCA framework).

### General qualifications of a treaty investor

To qualify for E-2 classification, the treaty investor must:

- Be a national of a country with which the United States maintains a treaty of commerce and navigation, or qualifying international agreement, or which has been deemed a qualifying country by legislation;
- Have invested, or be actively in the process of investing, a substantial amount of capital in a bona fide enterprise in the United States;
- Be seeking to enter the United States solely to develop and direct the investment enterprise. This is established by showing at least 50 percent ownership of the enterprise or possession of operational control through a managerial position or other corporate device.

### "Investment" defined

An investment is the treaty investor's placing of capital, including funds and other assets, at risk in the commercial sense with the objective of generating a profit. The capital must be subject to partial or total loss if the investment fails. The treaty investor must show that the funds have not been obtained, directly or indirectly, from criminal activity.

### "Substantial amount of capital"

A substantial amount of capital is:

- Substantial in relationship to the total cost of either purchasing an established enterprise or establishing a new one;
- Sufficient to ensure the treaty investor's financial commitment to the successful operation of the enterprise;
- Of a magnitude to support the likelihood that the treaty investor will successfully develop and direct the enterprise. The lower the cost of the enterprise, the higher, proportionately, the investment must be to be considered substantial.

### "Bona fide enterprise"

A bona fide enterprise refers to a real, active, and operating commercial or entrepreneurial undertaking which produces services or goods for profit. It must meet applicable legal requirements for doing business within its jurisdiction.

### Marginality

The investment enterprise may not be marginal. A marginal enterprise is one that does not have the present or future capacity to generate more than enough income to provide a minimal living for the treaty investor and his or her family. An enterprise that lacks the capacity to generate such income but that has a present or future capacity to make a significant economic contribution is not a marginal enterprise. The projected future capacity should generally be realizable within five years from the date the alien commences the normal business activity of the enterprise.

### General qualifications of the employee of a treaty investor

To qualify for E-2 classification, the employee of a treaty investor must:

- Be the same nationality of the principal alien employer (who must have the nationality of the treaty country);
- Meet the definition of "employee" under relevant law; and
- Either be engaging in duties of an executive or supervisory character, or, if employed in a lesser capacity, have special qualifications.

If the principal alien employer is not an individual, it must be an enterprise or organization at least 50 percent owned by persons in the United States who have the nationality of the treaty country. Those owners must be maintaining nonimmigrant treaty investor status. If the owners are not in the United States, they must be, if they were to seek admission to this country, classifiable as nonimmigrant treaty investors.

### Period of stay

Qualified treaty investors and employees will be allowed a maximum initial stay of two years. Requests for extension of stay in, or changes of status to, E-2 classification may be granted in increments of up to two years each. There is no limit to the number of extensions an E-2 nonimmigrant may be granted. All E-2 nonimmigrants, however, must maintain an intention to depart the United States when their status expires or is terminated.

An E-2 nonimmigrant who travels abroad may generally be granted, if determined admissible by a U.S. Customs and Border Protection officer, an automatic two-year period of readmission when returning to the United States. It is generally not necessary to file a new Form I-129 with USCIS in this situation.

### Terms and conditions of E-2 status

A treaty investor or employee may only work in the activity for which he or she was approved at the time the classification was granted. An E-2 employee, however, may also work for the treaty organization's parent company or one of its subsidiaries as long as the:

- Relationship between the organizations is established;
- Subsidiary employment requires executive, supervisory, or essential skills; and
- Terms and conditions of employment have not otherwise changed.

USCIS must approve any substantive change in the terms or conditions of E-2 status. A "substantive change" is defined as a fundamental change in the employer's basic characteristics, such as, but not limited to, a merger, acquisition, or major event which affects the treaty investor or employee's previously approved relationship with the organization. The treaty investor or enterprise must notify USCIS by filing a new Form I-129 with fee, and may simultaneously request an extension of stay for the treaty investor or affected employee. The Form I-129 must include evidence to show that the treaty investor or affected employee continues to qualify for E-2 classification.

It is not required to file a new Form I-129 to notify USCIS about non-substantive changes. A treaty investor or organization may seek advice from USCIS, however, to determine whether a change is considered substantive. To request advice from USCIS, the treaty investor or organization must file Form I-129 with fee and a complete description of the change.

### Family of E-2 treaty investors and employees

Treaty investors and employees may be accompanied or followed by spouses and unmarried children who are under 21 years of age. Their nationalities need not be the same as the treaty investor or employee. These family members may seek E-2 nonimmigrant classification as dependents and, if approved, generally will be granted the same period of stay as the employee.

Spouses of E-2 workers in valid E-2 or E-2S status are considered employment authorized incident to status, except for spouses of long-term investors in the CNMI, who are required to apply for employment authorization per 8 CFR 274a.12(c)(12). Beginning January 30, 2022, USCIS and CBP began issuing Forms I-94 with COA codes E-1S, E-2S, E-3S, and L-2S to distinguish employment-authorized dependent spouses from dependent children. An unexpired Form I-94 notated with E-2S is acceptable as List C evidence of employment authorization.

If the family members are already in the United States and are seeking change of status to or extension of stay in an E-2 dependent classification, they may apply by filing a single Form I-539, Application to Extend/Change Nonimmigrant Status.

## 8. Adjudicator's Field Manual (AFM): Status and Use After Retirement

Crosswalk PDF (AFM to Policy Manual): https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/policy-manual-afm/afm-pmcrosswalk.pdf
Archived AFM chapter excerpts hosted by USCIS: https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/policy-manual-afm/ (browse by chapter)
Related FOIA document: https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/foia/TradersInvestors.pdf (Nonimmigrant Treaty Traders and Investors and Immigrant Investors)

### Status

USCIS retired the Adjudicator's Field Manual in May 2020 and migrated AFM content into the USCIS Policy Manual. The AFM is no longer the operative USCIS adjudication guide. Where remaining AFM content has not yet been folded into the Policy Manual, USCIS has posted PDFs of the legacy AFM chapters on its website and made a crosswalk available that maps each AFM chapter to its current Policy Manual location. To the extent any remaining AFM text conflicts with current Policy Manual content or Policy Memoranda, the Policy Manual prevails.

### Where E classification was located in the AFM

E classification was historically covered in AFM Chapter 32 (Treaty Traders and Treaty Investors). Some practitioner sources also reference AFM Chapter 33 or 34 for adjacent material on employment categories. AFM Chapter 34 ("Other Employment-Based Classifications") has been archived at: https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/policy-manual-afm/afm34-external.pdf

### How practitioners cite the AFM today

Where the USCIS Policy Manual is silent on a specific E-2 question that the AFM had addressed, practitioners cite the archived AFM PDF as persuasive (not controlling) authority, while flagging that the AFM is retired. The Policy Manual now controls. For most current E-2 issues, the Policy Manual Volume 2, Part G is sufficient. AFM-only citations are rarely necessary in 2026; they are most useful in narrow historical interpretive contexts or to track the genealogy of a USCIS interpretation.

### FOIA-archive option

Where a specific historical AFM provision is needed and the live USCIS PDF link is broken, the AFM in redacted public form is available through USCIS FOIA library: https://www.uscis.gov/records/electronic-reading-room. Request via Form G-639 if not posted.

## 9. AAO Non-Precedent Decisions: Access, Search, and Use in E-2 Practice

Source URL (search portal): https://www.uscis.gov/administrative-appeals/aao-decisions/aao-non-precedent-decisions
Parent (all AAO decisions): https://www.uscis.gov/administrative-appeals/aao-decisions
AAO office page: https://www.uscis.gov/about-us/organization/directorates-and-program-offices/the-administrative-appeals-office-aao
AAO Practice Manual, Chapter 1 (overview): https://www.uscis.gov/administrative-appeals/aao-practice-manual/chapter-1-the-administrative-appeals-office
AAO decision data: https://www.uscis.gov/administrative-appeals/aao-decision-data
Form I-290B (Notice of Appeal or Motion): https://www.uscis.gov/i-290b

### What they are and what they are not

The AAO is the administrative appellate body within USCIS. The vast majority of its decisions are non-precedent decisions, which apply existing law and policy to the facts of a given case. Non-precedent decisions are not binding on subsequent adjudications, but they are widely used by USCIS officers, by AAO panels, and by practitioners as persuasive authority showing how the AAO has analyzed comparable fact patterns. Precedent designation is reserved to USCIS / DHS by separate process (28 C.F.R. 1003.1(g) analog); precedent AAO decisions are designated in the I&N Decision reporter.

### Where to search

The USCIS AAO Non-Precedent Decisions portal (above) provides a searchable index. Search options include:

- Free-text search across the body of decisions;
- Subject-matter or petition-type filters (e.g., I-129 for nonimmigrant petitions);
- Date range;
- Unique case identification number if known.

For E-2 specifically, the most effective search filter is form type "Form I-129" with text "E-2" or "treaty investor." Many E-2 decisions are organized under the "B7 - Nonimmigrant Treaty Traders and Investors" category if the portal uses that grouping; the portal's category labels have shifted over the years.

### Recent posting status (2025-2026 access)

USCIS stopped posting newly issued AAO decisions on the public portal in March 2025 without an explanation, which led to litigation by the American Immigration Council and partners. Pursuant to a March 30, 2026 court order, USCIS is required to publish past AAO non-precedent decisions and any new decisions issued by the AAO by November 30, 2026. Practitioners should expect that, as of mid-2026, the searchable archive will be more complete and current than it was during the March 2025 to early 2026 gap. For decisions issued during the gap period, FOIA via Form G-639 is the fallback. See https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/foia-request/restoring-access-to-aao-decisions/ for litigation background.

### How to use AAO non-precedent decisions in E-2 practice

- They are persuasive, not binding. Cite as "AAO non-precedent decision, [case ID], [date]," and note their non-precedent status.
- They are most useful for showing the AAO's analytical pattern on issues that recur: source-of-funds adequacy, marginality five-year projections, characterization of "irrevocably committed" funds, distinction between bona fide enterprises and shells/idle real estate holdings, the executive/supervisory analysis for employees, and ownership/control through corporate devices (holding companies, trusts).
- Pair AAO citations with the controlling authority: INA 101(a)(15)(E)(ii), 8 CFR 214.2(e), USCIS Policy Manual Volume 2, Part G, and where consular, 9 FAM 402.9.

### Examples of recurring E-2 issues in AAO non-precedent decisions

Without endorsing any specific decision as controlling, recurring AAO E-2 themes include:

- Failure to establish that funds were irrevocably committed (e.g., funds still held in petitioner's personal account at filing);
- Marginality denials where five-year projections rely on speculative revenue assumptions;
- Insufficient evidence that the petitioner will "develop and direct" (passive investment posture);
- Employee qualification denials where claimed essential skills are readily available in the U.S. labor market;
- Nationality / ownership denials where corporate chain ownership doesn't trace 50% to the claimed treaty country.

For an active filing, run the AAO search on the precise factual issue presented and pull the three to five most analytically relevant non-precedent decisions for argumentation.

### Filing an AAO appeal

Most appeals must be filed on Form I-290B, Notice of Appeal or Motion, with fee, within 30 calendar days after personal service of the decision (33 calendar days if mailed). The initial USCIS field office conducts an "initial field review" and may either treat the appeal as a motion to reopen/reconsider and grant the benefit, or forward the appeal and the record of proceedings to the AAO for a new decision.
